# Maps

A map in this project is two things that are kept deliberately separate:

- **the plate** — a drawn map, committed as artwork and never edited by any tool here
- **the board** — `data/maps/<id>.json`, which says what is *on* that artwork, hex by hex

The plate is a picture. The board is the rules' view of it. The hex grid is an overlay
that belongs to the board and is drawn on top of the plate at read time, so the artwork
survives untouched and the board can be corrected without a repaint.

The first map is **The Korvane Reach**, in [`korvane-reach.png`](korvane-reach.png), with
its board in [`data/maps/korvane-reach.json`](../../data/maps/korvane-reach.json).

| | |
| --- | --- |
| [**index.html**](index.html) | pan, zoom, click a hex, toggle the layers, download it |
| [**print.html**](print.html) | tile it across A4 sheets and print it, or save it as a PDF |
| `export.js` | turns the overlay into a file — PNG and SVG — for both of the above |
| `<id>-proof.png` | the whole board as one picture — generated by `tools/build-map.mjs`, git-ignored, for review |

## The board format

```json
"grid":   { "cols": 32, "rows": 26, "orientation": "pointy", "offset": "odd-r" },
"legend": { "~": "deep-water", "-": "shallow-water", "c": "coast", ... },
"rows":   [ "~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~",
            "~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~-----~~~~", ... ]
```

**One character per hex, one string per row.** It is the most important decision in the
format and it is not a compression trick: it means the board is a picture of itself in a
text editor, an edit is a one-character diff, and a code review of a coastline is
readable. The alternative — 832 objects with `col`, `row` and `terrain` — is the same
information in a form nobody can see.

Every character must appear in `legend`, and every id in `legend` must exist in
[`data/terrain.json`](../../data/terrain.json). Rows are `grid.rows` long and every row is
`grid.cols` characters. `tools/validate-map.mjs` enforces all of that.

Coordinates are **offset, odd-r**: `col,row` from `0,0` at the top left, with odd rows
shifted half a hex right. Hexes are pointy-top. `tools/lib/hexgrid.mjs` and
`docs/map/map.js` are the two implementations — one for the tools, one for the browser —
and they are duplicated on purpose, because `docs/` has to work when you double-click a
file off disk and a browser will not import a module from outside that directory.

### What else the board carries

- **`plate`** — where the artwork is, how big it is, and `field`: the drawn map inside the
  printed frame, in plate pixels. Every position in the file is a fraction of `field`, so
  the board survives the plate being redrawn at a different resolution.
- **`plate.occlusions`** — rectangles where map furniture (a title cartouche, a legend
  panel, a compass rose) is drawn over the map. The tracer refuses to read terrain from
  these and says how many hexes it had to guess.
- **`settlements`** — `col`, `row`, `rank` (`seat`, `city`, `town`, `village`) and
  `harbour`. A harbour must stand on a shore; the validator checks it.
- **`regions`** — named areas with a label anchor. Descriptive: the terrain lives in
  `rows`, and a region does not duplicate it.
- **`routes`** — `rail` and `shipping` are hex paths, because the rules price
  infrastructure per tile and a path has to be walkable. `road` is a list of links between
  settlement ids, because the plate draws roads as connections rather than as a surveyed
  line, and inventing a hex path for each one would be putting detail in the data that is
  not on the map.
- **`print`** — sheet layouts. `mapWidthMm`, `mapHeightMm` and `hexAcrossFlatsMm` are
  derived from the geometry and rewritten by `tools/build-map.mjs`; they are stored so the
  numbers are greppable and so a change to the grid shows up as a diff.

## Printing it

`print.html` tiles the whole plate across a grid of A4 sheets, puts trim marks on the four
corners of each sheet's window and stamps every sheet with its row and column. Print at
**100%** — "fit to page" silently rescales and the pieces will not meet — then cut on the
marks and butt the sheets edge to edge.

| Preset | Sheets | Finished | Hex | Resolution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `four-sheet` | 4, A4 landscape, 2 × 2 | 549 × 388 mm (A2) | 16.7 mm | 162 dpi |
| `nine-sheet` | 9, A4 landscape, 3 × 3 | 823 × 582 mm (A1) | 25.1 mm | 108 dpi |
| `one-sheet` | 1, A4 landscape | 274 × 194 mm | 8.4 mm | 325 dpi |

**Why every preset is a square grid of landscape sheets.** A4 is a root-two rectangle and
so is this plate, and the margin does not spoil it: an A4 printed landscape inside an 8 mm
margin leaves a 281 × 194 mm window, aspect 1.448, against the plate's 1.414. Those two
nest to within 2%, so a grid that is *n* sheets to a side reproduces the plate's shape at
any *n* and fills the paper — 98% of it, with the 6.6 mm that is left over going to the
right-hand margin. One, four and nine sheets are the same window repeated 1, 2 and 3 to a
side; nothing about a sheet changes between presets except how many of them there are.

Grids that are not square do not work, and six sheets has no square arrangement. A 3 × 2
portrait block is 582 × 562 mm, aspect 1.036 — nothing like the plate — so the map is
pinned by the width and only 73% of the paper carries any ink: 83 mm at the foot of all six
pages is blank. The other six-sheet arrangements are worse (3 × 2 landscape 65%, 2 × 3
landscape 68%, 2 × 3 portrait 33%). Six sheets did ship, printing 582 × 411 mm, and was
replaced by `four-sheet`: 6% smaller, two fewer sheets, and the paper actually used.

If four sheets is the size you want but you would rather have the extra 6%, eight sheets in
a 4 × 2 *portrait* block is the other efficient shape — 776 × 549 mm at 98% — because 4/2
is the ratio that makes a portrait block root-two the way 1/1 does for a landscape one.

If you want it genuinely bigger, use `nine-sheet`. The limit there is the plate: 3508
pixels across is 108 dpi at A1, which is soft up close and fine at arm's length on a table.
A plate drawn at 7000 px would print A1 at 210 dpi.

A sheet is 297 mm wide printed landscape, which is 1123 CSS pixels and wider than a laptop
window. The preview scales to fit and can be set to 100%, 50% or 25%; the scale is a screen
affordance only, undone both by the print stylesheet and again on `beforeprint`, because a
sheet that reached paper at 86% is a set of sheets that will not meet.

## Taking a copy away

Three ways out, and which one you want depends on whether the thing at the other end is
paper or a screen.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **PDF** | print from `print.html` and choose *Save as PDF*. The `@page` size is already set per preset, so the PDF is at true size and the trim marks are where they say they are. |
| **PNG** | *Download PNG*, on either page. The plate at its own 3508 × 2480, with whatever layers are switched on drawn over it. |
| **SVG** | *Overlay SVG*, on the viewer. The grid, routes, places and replacement legend as vector, at plate coordinates, without the plate — a few hundred kilobytes rather than ten megabytes, and the thing to edit if you want to redraw part of the overlay. |

**There is deliberately no per-sheet PNG.** A PNG carries no paper size with it, so a tile
exported from one of the sheets is an image nothing downstream can place at a known scale —
which is the one guarantee this page exists to make. Paper and PDF come off the sheets; the
PNG is one image of the whole map, for a screen, a virtual tabletop, or a print shop that
will set the size themselves.

Downloading reads the pixels back out of a canvas, and a browser will not allow that when
the page was opened off disk — over `file://` every image counts as a foreign origin. The
buttons say so rather than failing silently. Everything else on both pages works fine from
disk; only the downloads need the site to be served.

The exported look is defined once, in `export.js`, so a file saved from the viewer and one
saved from the print sheets are the same picture. It has to be stated there in full because
a file that has left the browser carries no stylesheet with it: the custom properties are
spelled out, `vector-effect` is dropped — a hairline that ignores zoom is a screen
affordance and would come out as a thread at 3508 px — and the selection highlight is
dropped, because which hex you last clicked is not part of the map.

### The legend is replaced, not reprinted

The plate's own key names four things this game has no terrain for — *tundra & steppe* is
two terrains, *desert & dunes* is two more, and hills and marsh do not appear on it at all.
So the overlay covers that one panel and prints the twelve real terrains in the same space,
with their move costs, alongside every mark from the original key. The artwork is not
edited; the panel is simply drawn over.

## Making a new map

Six steps. Steps 1 and 4 need a person or a model with judgement; the rest are mechanical.

### 1. Get a plate

Commission or generate the artwork. The brief is at the end of this document. Save it to
`docs/map/<id>.png`. Landscape, and as many pixels as you can get — the plate's width is
the hard limit on print quality, and 7000 px is the difference between a map you can read
at A1 and one you cannot.

### 2. Measure it

Create `data/maps/<id>.json` with `plate` (file, width, height, `field`), `grid`,
`legend`, `print` and empty `rows`. `field` is the drawn map inside the decorative frame,
in pixels; measure it off the inner frame rule. List every piece of map furniture drawn
over the field in `plate.occlusions` as fractions of `field`.

**Choosing `grid.cols` is the one real judgement call.** The flat-to-flat width of a hex is
exactly `field.width / cols`. Too few columns and named places share hexes and mountain
ranges collapse to single tiles; too many and a hex is too small to stand a marker in. The
Korvane Reach uses 32, which puts every one of its nineteen settlements in a hex of its own
and keeps a hex about two mountain glyphs across. Fifteen columns was tried first and lost
half the map's detail.

### 3. Trace it

```bash
node tools/trace-map.mjs <id>            # print the proposed rows
node tools/trace-map.mjs <id> --write    # write them into the board
```

The tracer samples the artwork under every hex. It can honestly separate five things by
colour — water, snow, grass, wood and sand — finds mountains by how much black ink is in
the hex, and finds rivers by looking for the drawn river blue. Then it applies the rules
that are true of any map: the sea is flooded in from the border so that inland water comes
out as lake rather than ocean, deep water beside land becomes shallow, and open ground
against the sea becomes coast.

It will get things wrong, and the two ways it goes wrong are worth knowing in advance:

- **Display lettering reads as mountains.** Black serif capitals a centimetre tall are, to
  a sampler, exactly as inky as a hatched peak. `THE AMBER STEPPE` grew a mountain range
  across flat grassland.
- **Two terrains can share a colour.** On this plate a fen and a desert are painted the
  same ochre and drawn with the same glyph. Nothing in the pixels can separate them; only
  the label can, and the label is prose.

### 4. Correct it by hand

This is the step that matters and it is not optional. Edit `rows` directly.

```bash
node tools/build-map.mjs <id>     # writes docs/map/<id>-proof.png
node tools/trace-map.mjs <id> --diff   # where the board and the plate now disagree
```

The proof sheet is the artwork with every hex tinted by its terrain, lettered with its
character, and ruled every fifth column and row. Open it, find what is wrong, fix the
string, rebuild. Expect three or four rounds. It is deliberately **not** committed — it is
two megabytes that would land in history again every time somebody fixed one hex. Review a
map change in `index.html`, or generate the proof and look at that.

`--diff` is for later: run it when someone has edited the board or replaced the plate, to
see whether the two still describe the same world.

### 5. Validate it

```bash
node tools/validate-map.mjs
```

Beyond ids and lengths it checks the things a hex map can check about itself: no deep water
touching land, no coast without water beside it, no lake shore without a lake, no town
standing in the sea, no harbour inland, no rail crossing water without a bridge, no
shipping lane over dry ground, and every route step landing on a neighbour. It also counts
the landmasses and reports the terrain mix against `boardSetup.terrainMix` in
`data/terrain.json` — never as an error, because a drawn continent is not a shuffled tile
bag, but a map with a quarter of the hills the rules assume is a map where iron is scarce,
and that is worth knowing before anyone plays on it.

### 6. Build and commit

```bash
node tools/build-map.mjs <id>    # proof sheet, and the derived print sizes
node tools/build-data.mjs        # docs/data/bundle.js, which the web pages read
```

Commit the plate, the board and the rebuilt bundle — not the proof, which is git-ignored.
Nothing needs to be added to `data/manifest.json`: maps are picked up from the directory,
so the next board is one new file.

## Terrain vocabulary

**The game's twelve terrains are the vocabulary. A map does not get to add to it.**

Drawn maps name their country the way a cartographer does — *steppe*, *highlands*, *fens*,
*dunes*, *waste* — and those names do not line up with a rules table. When they collide,
**the map changes and `data/terrain.json` does not.** A thirteenth terrain added to serve
one map is a rule every other map, every recipe and every building has to know about
forever.

Resolving a collision has an order:

1. **Take the name at its word if the game has that terrain.** "Varl Highlands" is drawn as
   flat steppe and named a highland. It became `hills`. "The Mirewash Fens" is painted the
   same ochre as the desert beside it, drawn with the same glyph, named a fen, and has
   three rivers running through it. It became `marsh`.
2. **Otherwise map the drawing onto the nearest terrain.** The Amber Steppe is grassland.
   The dunes on the northern peninsula are a sandy `coast`, not a desert — a dune field
   next to an ice waste is a beach.
3. **Split a legend entry that covers two terrains.** This plate's key says "tundra &
   steppe" for one symbol; the board reads the pale north as `tundra` and the yellow-green
   middle as `grassland`, and the replacement legend prints them as the two separate things
   they are.
4. **Never invent a terrain, and never stretch one to cover something it is not.** If a map
   genuinely needs ground the game does not have, that is a design decision about the game,
   made in `data/terrain.json` with the recipes, buildings and deposits that go with it —
   not a map fix.

Record every one of these calls in the `regions` entry for the area. The Korvane Reach's
Varl Highlands and Mirewash Fens both say in their summary that the plate draws one thing
and names another, and that the board follows the name.

### The coastline rule

Do not convert every land hex touching the sea into `coast`. On a continent as sea-riddled
as this one that repaints a fifth of the land a single colour and throws away exactly what
the drawing was read for. Convert open ground; leave an ice shelf as `tundra`, a dune field
as `desert` and a wooded shore as `forest`, and place harbours by hand where the plate
actually draws one.

## Brief for generating a new plate

Read [`../art/07-ai-agent-brief.md`](../art/07-ai-agent-brief.md) first — the palette, the
two-plate system and the banned list all apply. This is the map-specific part.

A generated map is only useful if it hexes cleanly afterwards, and everything below exists
to make step 3 above work rather than to make the picture prettier.

### Prompt

```text
A hand-drawn fantasy region map on aged parchment, in the style of a 17th-century
printed atlas plate: fine engraved coastline, hand-lettered place names in a
serif face, a decorative border rule, a title cartouche in one corner, a compass
rose, a scale bar in leagues, and a legend panel in another corner.

Flat muted washes of colour with hard edges and no blending: dusty grey-green
for woodland, pale yellow-green for open grass, warm ochre for sand and dry
country, near-white for ice and tundra, muted slate blue for the sea, with the
shallows a lighter tint of the same blue than the deep water. Paper is warm
oatmeal, never white. Line is warm near-black, never pure black.

Landforms are drawn as repeated small engraved glyphs on top of the wash:
hatched triangular peaks for mountains, small arcs for hills, tiny conifers and
round-topped trees for forest, low tufts for tundra, horizontal tufts for marsh,
loose crescents for dunes. Rivers are thin cold-blue lines running from high
ground to the sea. Roads are thin double lines, railways are hatched lines.

One landmass filling most of the frame, with open sea on at least two edges,
a deeply indented coastline, offshore islands, an inland lake or two, one
mountain range crossing the interior, and fifteen to twenty settlements varying
from a walled city to a village.

Strictly no gradients, no glow, no drop shadow, no soft airbrushed shading, no
photographic texture, no 3D relief or hillshading.
```

### Negative prompt

Use the one in the art brief, and add:

```text
hillshading, terrain relief shading, satellite imagery, contour lines, grid
lines, hex grid, square grid, latitude longitude lines, modern typography,
sans-serif labels, national borders, political colouring, minimap, game UI
```

`hex grid` is in there deliberately. **Never ask for a grid on the plate.** The grid is an
overlay drawn from the board; a grid baked into the artwork cannot be moved, cannot be
re-sized when `cols` changes, and will not line up with the one the tools draw.

### The seven rules a plate has to follow to be traceable

1. **Landscape, and as large as possible.** 4000 px wide is a working minimum; 7000 is
   what A1 wants.
2. **Flat washes, hard edges.** Every gradient, glow or hillshade is a region the sampler
   reads as two terrains, or as one it has no name for.
3. **One wash per terrain, and no two terrains sharing one.** This is the rule this plate
   breaks — its fens and its desert are the same ochre — and it cost the most hand
   correction. A fen that is grey-green instead of ochre traces itself.
4. **Water colder than every land colour.** The tracer separates sea from land by
   red-versus-blue, and it is the one test on the whole plate that never misfires.
5. **Keep display lettering off the terrain you care about, or accept that it will read as
   mountains.** Put the big region names over sea, ice, or open ground you are happy to
   correct by hand.
6. **Map furniture in the corners, over water.** A cartouche over open sea costs nothing;
   one over a coastline hides the coastline, and no amount of voting from the ring of hexes
   around it will tell you where that coastline ran.
7. **A plain frame with a visible inner rule.** That rule is what `plate.field` is measured
   from.

### After generating

Go to step 2 and keep going. A plate is not finished when the picture looks right; it is
finished when `validate-map.mjs` is clean and the proof sheet matches the drawing.
