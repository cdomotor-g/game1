# 08 — What is in the box

A first pass at the physical bill of materials, generated from the data. Quantities are
for a 4-player game and are estimates, not a manufacturing spec.

## Board

> **The 61 terrain tiles are shelved — [#18](https://github.com/cdomotor-g/game1/issues/18).** They stay in the bill
> of materials because they are still the right answer for a *tile set*; the board
> currently being built is one printed map plate — see the Maps section of the
> root README. Deposit and tree tokens are unaffected: they sit on a hex whether
> that hex is printed on a tile or on a map. The **building tiles** below are a
> separate line and are not shelved: they go *on* whichever board the table has.

- **61 hex tiles** — double-sided, terrain printed on the face, a neutral "unexplored"
  pattern on the back. Terrain mix in `data/terrain.json` under `boardSetup.terrainMix`.
- **45 deposit tokens** — placed face down under tiles at setup, flipped when surveyed.
  Counts per type in `data/deposits.json` (`tokensInSetup`).
- **~40 tree tokens** — forest tiles carry 6 each; removed as they are felled.

## Building tiles

Placed on the board, or on a mini-map sheet opened out from one of its hexes. One cell of a
tile is exactly one world-map hex, so a piece cut for one board seats on the other without
being trimmed. Generated by `tools/build-tiles.mjs` into [`../tiles/`](../tiles/index.html);
how big each one is comes out of `data/buildingtiles.json` and is never typed on a building.

- **54 tiles, double-sided, 108 drawn plates** — 49 buildings and 5 sown fields, each drawn
  twice. Face is the building finished; back is the same ground with the work not yet done,
  and the tile is laid that way up while the work is being paid for. Both sides carry the
  tile's own name — the picture is what tells the two apart.
- **21 one-cell, 21 two-cell, 10 three-cell, 2 four-cell** — 16.7 × 19.3 mm up to
  41.8 × 33.8 mm at the current print preset. A hut is one cell; a manor and a pasture are
  four.
- **How many of each** is a supply question, not a deck: three or four of the cheap tier-1
  tiles per player, one or two of anything above tier 2, one of anything unique.
- **Road, rail and bridge are not tiles.** They are laid along a route rather than placed on
  a cell, and they are already a piece — the route bars in `components.json tokens.route`.

## Per player (×4)

- **1 player board** — A4 landscape, and every one identical. A recess for the card in
  play, four more for whatever that card has in play, four numbered tracks up the middle,
  a narrow wear ladder against each of the four kit recesses, and the round's phases
  underneath. Print more than there are players: one spare as the encounter board, and one
  for every vehicle on the road. Generated by
  `tools/build-board.mjs` into [`docs/boards/`](../boards/index.html); the anatomy is
  below, under **The player board**
- **20 building markers** in the player colour
- **12 worker meeples**, 4 specialist meeples (distinct shape), 6 soldier meeples
- **4 figures** — prospector, merchant, hero, and a spare
- **6 tool cards**, and nothing slides on them any more. The sliding durability marker was
  the fiddliest component in the box and the one most worth prototyping early; it was
  retired not by solving it but by the change that gave a sword a wear number. A tool is
  now the same kind of object as a weapon — a card that lies in a kit slot, printing its
  wear once as a maximum in the **W** box, with a pip walking the ladder beside it. The
  fiddliest component in the box is now a 4.5 mm disc.
- **30 road segments**, 20 rail segments, 4 bridges
- **6 effort dice** — d6 standard, plus a d4/d8/d10/d12 set shared between players for
  ladder effects

## Cards

- **69 event cards** — the deck in `data/events.json`
- **66 commodity reference cards** — one per commodity, showing value, bulk, and what
  makes it. These double as the market price reference.
- **87 recipe cards** — or, more likely, 8 double-sided player reference sheets grouped
  by category. 87 cards is a lot of shuffling for something nobody shuffles.
- **29 item cards**, **14 weapon cards**, **5 armour cards** and **6 talisman cards** —
  one file (`data/items.json`) and four decks. Weapons and armour left the items deck
  because they had stopped being items: an item is a thing you carry and these are two
  numbers you add to a battle total, and a player reaching for a sword should not be
  shuffling past a satchel, a coil of rope and sixteen potions to find one. What is left
  in ITEMS is what the word actually means — clothing, lights, potions, and the gear a
  hand carries: rope, hook, bag, satchel, binoculars.
- **12 monster cards**, **12 vehicle cards**, **8 character cards**, **8 quest cards** —
  the adventure decks, in `data/monsters.json`, `vehicles.json`, `characters.json` and
  `quests.json`, all built to grow

**Card codes.** Every card carries a deck prefix and a two-digit sequence — `VEH-03`,
`MON-09`, `QST-07` — with a `v2` suffix if a card is ever reprinted changed. The scheme
is data (`vehicles.json → cardIdScheme`), the numbering is this repository's own, and a
fork is free to renumber. Two prefixes are new: `WPN` and `ARM`, dealt when weapons and
armour became decks of their own. A card that moves between decks takes a new code rather
than keeping an old one, because the prefix is which deck to put it back in.

**The summary strip.** Every number a card has is printed once, as a **maximum**, in a
row of lettered boxes across the top of the card: Corin Vale is `H 10  S 3  M 0  ¤ 45
KG 9`, and a monster is `H  S  A  P  Y` and its element's mark in the last box. Six
boxes is what a 63 mm card holds at a legible size, and the letters are the **player
board's own track letters** wherever the board has one, so setting up is reading across
the strip and putting tokens down left to right. Nothing on a card moves. Everything that
moves is on the board.

There used to be a bar convention — a vertical numbered ladder up each edge, harm left and
capacity right, walked by a token. It was right for a card held in the hand and it stopped
being right the day the player board arrived, because **a card in a recess is a card whose
edges are under the board.** The tracks moved; the ladders became decoration that ate two
columns of picture; and a character with two capacities needed a third bar laid over their
own face. Taking them off gave the portrait the full width of the card and gave every deck
one geometry instead of four.

Where the element used to be a badge beside the name, pushing everything else a line down
the card, it is now the last box of the strip: the same ring and mark, in a box the size
of the numbers beside it.

**There is no D in the strip.** The letters are the board's track letters wherever a track
exists, and `tools/validate-data.mjs` fails the build if a card and a board ever call the
same number by different names — so when defence left the game the letter left the strip
with it. **A** took its place and is not the same promise: a track letter says something
moves, a card-only letter says nothing does. Armour is printed and added and never walked,
which is why it has a letter and no column. **W** went the other way in the same redesign —
it was the one printed maximum with no track under it and now has four.

**The backs.** One per deck, so a face-down stack is identifiable across the table without
turning a card over. Each is a single word — the deck's name — mirrored across the card's
horizontal centre line over an engine-turned ground, with a motif in a roundel on the axis
of symmetry. The word is the identification; the ink and the motif are the fast read at
arm's length, before anybody is close enough to spell anything.

| Deck | Word | Motif | Ink |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Characters | CHARACTERS | rivet | ochre |
| Vehicles | VEHICLES | wheel | slate |
| Monsters | MONSTERS | eye | oxide |
| Talismans | TALISMANS | rayed | bruise |
| Spells | SPELLS | rayed | bruise |
| Modifications | MODIFICATIONS | wheel | verdigris |
| Events | EVENTS | rivet | oxide |
| Quests | QUESTS | compass | ochre |
| Items | ITEMS | rivet | slate |
| **Weapons** | **WEAPONS** | **blade** | **oxide** |
| **Armour** | **ARMOUR** | **mail** | **slate** |
| Tools | TOOLS | socket | verdigris |

Twelve decks, eight motifs and five inks, and a motif is allowed to repeat because the ink
under it does not: rivet is a character in ochre, an event in oxide and an item in slate.
One pair shares both — **talismans and spells are each a rayed mark in bruise** — and there
the word is the entire distinction. That is the weak point of the convention rather than an
oversight in it: they are the two arcane decks, bruise is the arcane ink and it is rationed,
and giving one of them a second colour would be worse than the ambiguity. It is the pair to
look at first on a printed test sheet.

**The two new backs are new because the decks are.** WEAPONS is a **double-ended blade
through a crossguard** in oxide; ARMOUR is **five interlocked mail rings** in slate. Both are
top-to-bottom symmetric, and both had to be, because the back mirrors across the card's
centre line — a motif that is not symmetric is mirrored into nonsense. A sword is the one
object in this game *improved* by that constraint: point up, point down, quillons through
the middle, no way up, and it comes out reading as a court card. The guard is the widest
thing on the grid, so the silhouette is a cross before it is a sword, and a cross is what
carries at arm's length. Mail went the other way and is the only motif in the set built out
of **repeats** rather than out of one figure — a ring at the centre and one at each quarter,
overlapping — which is precisely what mail is: twenty thousand identical small pieces, and at
roundel size that is what it should look like.

## The player board

One A4 landscape sheet a player keeps in front of them. It does three jobs, and the
third is the one that earned it:

- **A place for the hero.** A recess the character card drops into, and four more for
  whatever else is in play — a weapon, a suit, a lantern, the axe, a talisman, an accepted
  quest. Four, not five: a hero who wants a fifth thing has to put something down, which is
  the same argument the strength limit is making — and it is a harder argument than it was,
  because every slot now has a wear ladder against it. A fifth thing in play is a fifth
  thing whose wear nobody is counting.
- **The turn reference**, printed from `rules.json` so it cannot fall out of step with
  the rules the way a hand-lettered one would.
- **Four numbered tracks up the middle and four narrow ladders at the edge**, and this is
  the point. Everything that moves in front of one player moves here, in one place, where
  it can be reached and knocked back to zero. The middle of the board is about the figure
  in the recess; the edge of it is about what that figure is carrying.

| | Track | A rung is | Runs | Walked by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **H** | Health | 1 | 0–14 | The figure, hurt and mended — and only medical aid mends it. A vehicle reads the same column as its hull |
| **S** | Strength | 1 | 0–14 | Set from the card; a rung down for every night without a camp, all of it back after one night's sleep |
| **P** | Pace | 1 hex | 0–14 | Hexes left in this leg — and what you have to beat to run from a monster |
| **M** | Mana | 1 | 0–14 | The body and every talisman in a slot |
| **W** ×4 | Wear | 1 | 0–14 | **One ladder per kit slot**, set from the card's W box, down a rung every time the thing is used, walked by a pip |

**Pace** is the board's own: a party counts hexes off a speed table every single round of
the game and has never had anywhere to count them — set the token at the start of a leg,
walk it down a rung a hex, halve it for a night leg under a lantern. At zero the leg is
over and the discovery roll is where you stopped. It is called pace rather than speed
because strength has the S.

**There is no V.** There was: a sixth column called Vehicle, counting damage on the wagon
the player was running — printed on every board in the game whether anybody was running one
or not, and the only number on the board that walked *up*. A vehicle is a figure now. It is
dealt a player board of its own and run like a player who is not a person: its card in the
recess, its cargo and its modifications in the four kit slots, and its **hull on the health
track**, set from the card, down as it is damaged, up as it is repaired, wrecked at nothing.
A hull is a body. The five columns left over got the width back.

**Strength** swallowed burden. They were the same arm doing the same job under two
numbers: what you swing with and what you can shoulder. One track now, and what a figure
carries is `strength × 3` kilograms — printed on the card so nobody multiplies at the
table, derived so it can never disagree with the strength beside it.

**And coin is on those scales now.** It used to be on the short list of things a figure
carried weightlessly — an abstraction that lived outside the world, and the one thing
anybody could hold an unlimited quantity of. A coin weighs **25 g**, which is forty to the
kilogram and is a real weight for a real coin: a sovereign is eight grams and a piece of
eight is twenty-seven. So a strength-3 figure, whose limit is 9 kg, could shoulder **360
coin and nothing else**, and the 60 they start with is a kilogram and a half — a rounding
error beside a sword. That is the shape the rule wants. It costs nothing for the whole
early game and it bites exactly once: the round a party sells a hold of jewellery and has
to work out how the money is getting home. Coin is not a number in the corner of the sheet
any more, it is cargo. It still walks no track, because kilograms do not.

**There is no D either.** Defence was a rating a token barely ever moved, and when it went
it took a whole column with it. It earned its place under the **to-hit roll** — strength
used to sit on *both* sides of that roll, which quietly made every strong thing armoured
for no better reason than that it hit hard — and it does not survive the roll it was
fixing. Once nobody is counting hits, a number that stops a blow landing and a number that
soaks it after it has are the same number: both are an amount by which the other side's
roll fails to hurt you, and the only thing that ever told them apart was *when* you applied
it. That bookkeeping is precisely what an opposed total deletes. So there is one number, it
is called **armour**, it is printed on the card and added to a total, and no token walks it.
See **A fight**, below.

**What the column paid for is at the edge of the sheet.** The D column came out of the
middle the same day the four wear ladders arrived beside the kit recesses, and the board
gained both at once: four columns at **15.6 mm** against the 13.6 that five of them
managed, with the wear ladders already taken out. Margins came in from 8 mm to 6 and
gutters from 6 to 4 to pay for the rest. None of that is luck — it is what deriving the
geometry is for. Take a column out of the middle, hand two millimetres back at the edges,
and the arithmetic gives the difference to whatever is left standing.

A wear ladder is drawn against its card rather than up the middle because **it is the first
number on this board that belongs to a card rather than to whoever is sitting behind it**. A
single W column could only ever have counted one thing's wear and a figure carries four, so
there are four of them, drawn where the thing they count is lying. That is also what changed
the piece: fifteen rungs down the side of an 88 mm recess is not quite six millimetres a
rung, which a 7 mm bar overhangs and a **4.5 mm pip** leaves a millimetre and a half to get a
fingernail under. Every fifth rung rules heavier and carries its figure and the rest are
plain cells, because numbering all fifteen at that width would be numbering nothing legibly.

**The tracks are numbered and nothing else.** No rung glyph, no plus, no minus. Each rung
used to carry a little ink-plate mark saying which *kind* of number it was, and at a shade
over 11 mm a column it was competing with the figure for the same three millimetres. Which
way a token walks is a sentence in the rulebook, where there is room to say it.

**Every track runs 0 to 14, and that is the game's ceiling as well as the board's.** The
zero rung matters: it is where a token starts and where it ends up, and a numbered rung
says so without a separate seat ring under the ladder. The top rung is a promise the
whole dataset has to keep — `data/playerboard.json` lists every number a token walks and
`tools/validate-data.mjs` sweeps the lot, so a fifteenth point of health fails the build
rather than walking off the top of a board somebody has already printed.

**Wear is on that ceiling now, and it got there by coming down.** It used to run to
thirty-four and be counted on the tool itself, because there was no track for it — and the
note beside the strip letter said there never would be one, in as many words: *it runs past
twenty, the board stops at fourteen.* There are four tracks for it now, so the number came
down to meet the board rather than the board going up to meet the number. Tools that ran
14–34 run **6–14**: the alembic is the frailest at 6 and a large loom the longest-lived
thing anybody owns at 14, which is the ceiling exactly, and what a figure carries on its
back sits under that — a torch is 1 and gone in a night, a plate harness 12. Nothing got
shorter, because the clock changed with the scale: an axe had 24 wear at one point a labour
*hour* and three hours to a job, which is eight jobs; it has 10 at one point a **job**,
which is ten. That is the identical trade the kilogram made when a hero could not stand
their own load on a fourteen-rung ladder, and it is the right way round both times — the
ceiling belongs to the game, and a number that will not fit under it is a number that is
wrong.

**Two numbers on a card are deliberately not on that list.** Kilograms never have to fit
the ceiling, because nothing walks a token for them: a load is totalled and checked against
the limit printed on the card, and a strength-6 caravan guard shouldering 18 kg is a
sentence, not a ladder — coin included, now that coin has mass. **Armour** is the other,
and it is the new one: a monster prints it in the **A** box, a character's is whatever gear
is lying in the kit slots, and either way it is added to a total and never walked. The
ceiling sweep leaves both alone for the same reason — a number belongs on that list only if
the board has a track for it, and the sweep is merciless about everything that does.

The tracks are the only thing on the board carrying colour: **harm in oxide, strength in
ochre, the leg in verdigris, mana in bruise, wear in slate**. Mana is the only arcane
subject on the sheet and the only thing that takes the slip.

**That is five inks doing a second job, because the first job is now dice.** The press has
five chromatic inks and the game has five colours of die, one in each — demand blue
(slate), supply red (oxide), volatility green (verdigris), spoil ochre, mana purple
(bruise); seven dice in the box, because demand and supply roll two apiece. So every ink on
the player board also names a die, and both claims stand because the two vocabularies never
share a sheet. The **market board holds no track** — it holds no state at all — and the
**player board draws no die**: the one place it names a colour is the IN A FIGHT panel at
its foot, which sets the words *blue* and *red* in plain ink beside the arithmetic, because
a die there is a thing you are about to roll and not a wash you are about to read. The
price ledger, the only sheet in the game that is written on, carries no chromatic ink at
all.

Where the two vocabularies do touch, once, it happens to help: **health washes in oxide and
oxide is the red die**, so the dice the thing in front of you rolls are the colour of the
track it takes off you. Wear in slate against blue — the die you roll yourself — means
nothing whatever, and is simply what was left after four pairings had been chosen for
reasons. What would break the arrangement is a die swatch printed on a player board an inch
from a track washed in the same ink, and nothing in the build would catch it, because
nothing in the build knows the two vocabularies are the same five inks.
`components.json marketBoard.dice.ink` is the single place they are tied together — five
lines — and it is the file to open first if a board ever wants a die drawn on it.

**One board, and every one identical — and print one more than there are players.**
Everything that differs between an orc and a halfling is printed on the card lying in the
recess, so the board underneath has no business knowing which of them is sitting there.
The spare board is the **encounter board**: when a discovery roll turns up a monster or a
stranger, their card is dealt onto it, its tracks are set from the card's summary strip,
and for the length of the encounter it is a player who is not a person. Being generic is
what makes that work.

**There is no border.** A card has a frame because a card is held, fanned and cut out; a
board is not. The 18 mm of paper the border used to take was doing nothing but making the
middle narrower, and taking it off is what paid for a sixth track — the columns came out
*wider* with six than they had been with five. Retiring that track, when a vehicle started
being dealt a board of its own, handed the width straight back: five columns, over 13 mm
each. Retiring defence handed it back again, and the margins and gutters were tightened on
top: **four columns at 15.6 mm, plus four wear ladders that were not there before**, and
not one coordinate typed to do any of it.

### A fight

**One subtraction, and it is the subtraction the market already taught you.** It is printed
on the board because it is the only arithmetic a player needs while a monster card is face
up in front of them, and it is printed from `rules.json conflict.battle` rather than
lettered on, so the board and the rulebook cannot drift:

> **Yours** = your strength + your gear + **two blue dice**.
> **Theirs** = its strength + its armour + **two red dice**.
> The lower total loses **health equal to the difference**. A tie wounds nobody.

Four things fall out of that, and the first is why the colours are what they are. This is
the identical gesture a player has already made once this round on the ledger — two blue
against two red, subtract, read the difference — so a table that has bought wheat has
already learned how a battle is scored, and nobody has to be told which pair is which.
Second, **nothing is counted**: no hits on the table between two players who each thought
the other was keeping the tally, no clamp that gets looked up twice a fight because it is
not obvious which way it runs. Third, **the board carries no number for it beyond health**,
because strength is on a column you already have and armour is printed on a card. And
fourth, the **exchange** is short by construction — one roll, one loser, one wound. A
*fight* is however many exchanges it takes, which is a different sentence and is
[question 12](10-open-questions.md).

**Gear is a number, and the four that matter each carry a rider.** A sword is +1; a steel
sword +2 and a **third blue die kept best-of-two**, which is the only place in the game
where more dice are rolled than are counted and is worth about a point and a half; a war
hammer +2 and it takes the *whole* of the other side's armour off their total, which is
nothing at all against a rime harpy at armour 0 and three whole points against Vhalrik;
plate is +3 and **−1 pace**, the one piece of gear that charges you on another track
entirely. And all of it takes **one wear point per round of battle**, both sides, win or
lose — a blow you turned still dented the plate — which is why every kit slot on the board
has a ladder against it.

**Worked, and it is the same worked example the board prints.** Strength 4 with a sword
(+1) and a leather jerkin (+1), rolling 7 on the blue, totals 13. A cinder wolf — strength
2, armour 1 — rolling 7 on the red, totals 10. The wolf takes 3, and it has 4 health, so one
more exchange finishes it. The same character against **Vhalrik**, strength 7 and armour 3,
who rolls 8: 18 against 13, and the character takes 5, half of everything they have. A
mismatch is a mauling, an even fight is a scratch, and the interesting decision is whether
to be in it at all — which is a **pace** question, not a combat one, and it is why half the
bestiary cannot be run away from.

### An encounter

A discovery roll that turns up a living thing has always produced a monster and never said
how to run one. It does now, and the answer is the furniture already on the table: **deal
its card onto the spare player board.** Set that board's tracks from the card's summary
strip — **health, strength, pace** — and it has everything a player has and is run the same
way, by whoever sits left of the player who drew it. Three of the strip's five boxes are
tracks; the other two are not, and that is the division the whole board runs on. **A** is
armour, which is added to a total and never moves, and **Y** is the most mana the thing's
death is worth, which is a ceiling on a die roll and not a payment. Neither gets a token,
because nothing about either of them changes while the encounter lasts. When it ends the
card is put away, the tokens come off, and nothing is written down. A stranger met on the
road is set up exactly like a hero, because that is exactly what they are to themselves.

## The market board

**Two A4 landscape sheets, and neither of them records a price.** That is the whole of what
changed. The board used to be a price tracker: six identical lines of tally, memory and
six-cell price ladder, three pieces to walk per line per round, and the entire apparatus
existed because the market models needed somewhere to remember things. They do not remember
anything now — a perishable's rule happens in a player's own hands at the end of the round,
a finite resource's number is read off its own grid, and a sought good's number is the move
it made last round, which is written on the ledger anyway. Nothing was left for six lines to
hold. So the price went to the **ledger**, where a price has belonged since people have had
prices, and the board became the thing a board is actually good at: one sheet, in front of
everybody, saying what the rules are.

**Sheet one, the market board. One per table** — it was one per town, because it held every
one of that town's prices, and a sheet that holds no prices is a sheet everybody can read at
once. A head and a body, and nothing stands on either.

| | Holds | Which is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Head** | THE ROLL | The dice key, the three-cell volatility strip, and the seven-cell swing ruler — the round left to right in the order it happens |
| **Body** | FOUR KINDS OF GOODS | One panel per pricing model, each headed by the mark engraved on that good's own token |
| **Foot** | THE MARKET | The prose, printed from `rules.json` rather than restated |

The dice are seven in five colours — two blue for demand, two red for supply, one green for
volatility, one ochre for spoil, one purple for mana — drawn as dice, on the wash plate for
the colour and on the ink plate for the outline and the face, so the black-and-white edition
still tells them apart. That matters more here than anywhere else in the game, because on
this sheet **the colour is the interface**.

The four panels are the sheet's whole subject, and they exist because a player who has
picked up a commodity token and does not know what the fish skeleton in its corner means
has to be able to find out without asking. **STAPLE** is the new one of the four, and it is
not a new rule — it is the absence of one, given a face and a panel, and thirty-four of the
sixty-six commodities run under it. A game where every good has a special rule is a game
where no good does.

**Sheet two, the depletion sheet.** Eighteen identical grids, nine across and two down, each
one three cells wide and seven rows deep under a hexagonal seat the commodity's own token
stands in. Fourteen commodities price by depletion, so one sheet covers a game with grids to
spare. A **pip** goes on the lowest uncovered cell every time a unit is *burnt* — not
traded — and the lowest number still showing is what that commodity adds to its swing. The
cell's printed figure is 3.2 mm inside a 4.5 mm pip, so a covered number has actually gone;
a figure peeping out from behind the piece that took it would make *the lowest one you can
still see* a matter of opinion, and `tools/build-market.mjs` fails the build rather than
print one.

It is a separate sheet for a reason that is not room. **It is spent.** At the end of a game
it is covered in pips that are out of the box for good, and it cannot be put away and got
out again the way every other component here can — so it should not be printed on the back
of something you keep.

Nothing on either sheet names a commodity, a town or a player, and nothing on the market
board is a place to put a piece at all. `tools/build-market.mjs` generates both from
`data/marketboard.json`, `data/pricing.json` and `rules.json`; the geometry is
`components.json marketBoard`, and there is not a coordinate in any of it. The head takes
the height its three strips need, the foot takes its rows, and the panels take everything
left divided by however many kinds of good `pricing.json` declares — so a fifth kind of good
narrows the panels and nothing runs off the paper. On the second sheet a cell is the pip
plus its clearance and a grid is the ladder in `pricing.json depletion`, so making a seam
last longer is one number there and a taller grid here.

## The price ledger

**One A4 portrait sheet, and the only place in the game where a price lives.** Six commodity
columns across, twenty-four rows down — one per round of the game — and every cell is three
hollow seven-segment figures with a small ruled box beside them. **One per game, per town
the table is actually trading in**, plus spares: a town dealing in more than six commodities
prints a second sheet, which is what a second sheet is for.

**Portrait, and the numbers chose it rather than taste.** Landscape gives 273 mm of width,
which is eleven columns nobody needs, and about 150 mm of history. Portrait gives 239 mm of
history and lands on six columns, which is a town's real traded list. A ledger wants rows.

**The figures are hollow and you colour them in.** That is stranger on paper than it sounds
and it is right for three reasons. A written number is somebody's handwriting and gets
argued about across a table, where a filled figure is a figure. Filling seven bars is fast,
and it is *exactly* as fast for 188 as for 8, which writing is not. And a wrong digit is
corrected by filling one more segment rather than by rubbing out, which is what lets the
whole sheet be worked at speed in pencil. All seven segments print whether they belong to
the number or not: an unlit segment is what makes a hollow figure read as a number
*waiting*, and drawing only the lit ones would mean the tool knew the price, which it must
not — the sheet is printed before anybody has rolled anything. Three digits, because the
dearest thing in the game is 110 at base and 220 at its top band, and a fourth digit would
be a column of noughts on every sheet ever printed. Leading zeros are left blank: a price of
8 is a blank, a blank and an 8, because nobody has ever written 008 on a ledger.

**No band strip and no token.** A band strip was tried at the head of a column and does not
fit — six cells of a 7 mm bar plus clearance is 56 mm against a 31 mm column — and it was
not worth widening the sheet for, because it was not worth having. Every commodity's six
prices are printed as one row of six figures in the annex, so a move is a **step along that
row**: last round's price is written in front of you, it is one of the six, the swing ruler
says how many places to step, and the figure you land on is the new price. Nothing is
multiplied and no band index is remembered. The head of a column is a hairline hexagonal
seat and nothing else — the token is the label, exactly as it was on the market lines this
sheet replaced, and a column is not the grain column until somebody stands the grain token
in it.

**The move box earns its 6 mm twice.** It is the one thing on the sheet that is written
rather than filled: the number of places the price just stepped, −3 to +3, or a dash if it
held. It is the **record** — a column of moves is the shape of a market at a glance, where a
column of prices is only its level — and for a **sought** good it is the *rule*, because the
hype modifier is the move box on the row above. That is how a market which runs on its own
reputation gets a memory without anything having to remember it, and it is what let the
memory strip come off the market board with nothing replacing it. A held round writes a
**dash** rather than a nought and rather than nothing: a dash says the roll happened and
came to nothing, an empty box says somebody forgot to roll this column, and those are
different problems.

A row is a **round**, not a price change — about three rounds in ten hold, so a column has
gaps in it and the gaps are the market holding. That is also what makes *the row above* mean
exactly one thing for the sought good's rule; in a free-running list a round that held would
write nothing and *the row above* would quietly mean two rounds ago.

`tools/build-ledger.mjs` generates it into [`docs/ledger/`](../ledger/index.html) from
`data/ledger.json` and `components.json ledger`. Every number on it is derived, and two of
them bind: a column is at least as wide as the token that heads it, and a row is the working
height over `rules.json victory.gameLengthRounds`. The current sheet is six columns of
31.17 mm by 24 rows of 10 mm, with digits 4.91 × 8.4 mm.

**The ledger and the depletion sheet are the only two components in the box that are
consumed by being used**, and they are consumed differently, which is worth saying out loud.
A ledger is spent because it is **written on**; a depletion sheet is spent because **pieces
are left on it**. Either way, neither can be put away at the end of the evening and got out
again — a used ledger is the record of somebody else's game and a used depletion sheet is a
seam that is already worked out. That makes them a different kind of object from everything
else here: every other component in this document is furniture, and these two are
stationery. So they are **printed fresh per game**, in pencil-ready ink, with spares, and
the ledger carries no chromatic ink at all — a pencil is the only colour it is going to get.

## Tokens and bits

- **Commodity tokens** — the big question. 63 commodities is far too many to give each
  its own wooden bit. Two candidate approaches:
  1. **Generic cubes in 11 category colours**, with the specific commodity tracked on a
     player-board grid. Cheap, compact, and it makes a stockpile hard to read at a
     glance from across the table.
  2. **Printed cardboard chits per commodity**, ~20 of the common ones and ~8 of the
     rare. Readable, thematic, and a genuinely large amount of punchboard.

  The digital prototype sidesteps this entirely, which is exactly why it should not be
  the thing that decides it. This needs a paper prototype before it needs an opinion.

  One thing the style guide settles either way: **eleven distinguishable colours do not
  exist** — not across colour-blindness, not across dye lots, and not in greyscale, where
  the eleven collapse into five clusters. So each category also carries a hatch pattern,
  which is what actually identifies it. Under option 1 the hatch moves to the
  player-board grid cell, and the cube supplies only colour — which is a real argument in
  favour of cubes, since it costs nothing to print a hatch into a cell.
  See [../art/02-palette.md](../art/02-palette.md).

  **Cut pieces are hexagons; struck pieces are discs.** A hexagon shares its cuts: nested
  on a laser bed, one straight line is the edge of two tokens, so a sheet of hexes yields
  around a fifth more pieces than the same sheet of discs and the head travels a good deal
  less. Commodity tokens are the ones there are hundreds of, so commodity tokens are
  **18 mm flat-top hexagons**, and deposits are the same shape at 24 mm — size is what
  tells them apart, since both live on the table.

  Whichever way it goes, a commodity token carries **two marks**: its family's, and the
  mark of **which of the four kinds of good it is** — staple, perishable, finite or sought —
  engraved small in the corner (`data/pricing.json`, drawn by `tools/build-icons.mjs`). It
  used to be one of three, and STAPLE joined them as the mark for the goods that do nothing
  special, which is thirty-four of the sixty-six: a balance hanging level, the only
  symmetrical mark in the set, because the other three all lean one way.

  The second mark is there because no sheet in the game can carry it. A ledger column is not
  about anything until a token is standing in its seat, so the rule that column runs under
  has to **arrive with the token** — and that is more load than it was, because the four
  models no longer share one strip on a board. Pick up grain and you are holding something
  that will rot in your hands at the end of the round; pick up iron ore and you are holding
  a hole in the ground that only deepens when somebody burns what comes out of it.

- **Coins** — round, because a coin that is not round is not a coin. There are far fewer of
  them, they are the one piece a hand finds by feel while the eyes are elsewhere, and round
  against hexagonal is the fastest distinction a hand can make in a heap. Three sizes —
  16 / 19 / 22 mm for 1 / 5 / 25 — so a stack is countable by feel and in greyscale. And a
  coin now weighs 25 g on the same scales as everything else a figure carries, which is the
  one property on this list that is a rule rather than a manufacturing note.
- **Bar tokens** — one small 7 mm disc per column track: **four per player board**, and
  **none on any market board**, because no market sheet has a strip left to walk. It was
  six, then five, then four — the vehicle column went when a wagon was dealt a board of its
  own, and the defence column went when a battle became one opposed total. None on any card
  either, because no card has a track.
- **Pips** — a 4.5 mm disc, the new piece, and it does **two jobs**. It walks a **wear**
  ladder beside a kit slot on the player board: four per board, and they come off again when
  the thing they were counting is finished with. And it covers a cell on a **depletion**
  grid: one per unit of a finite commodity burnt, laid down once and never lifted.

  **Four and a half millimetres, two and a half off a bar, and both numbers are doing a
  job.** A pip is the biggest piece a fifteen-rung wear ladder will carry without overhang,
  and leaves a millimetre and a half to get a fingernail under; a depletion cell is then
  sized off the pip in turn, so a grid is as small as the piece standing on it allows and
  eighteen of them fit on one sheet. One shape for two jobs is deliberate rather than
  thrifty: **a pip means one use of one thing has been spent**, whether the thing is an axe
  or a coal seam, and the two uses differ only in whether the spending can be undone. The
  piece has no business knowing which sheet it has been put on.

  **How many.** Four per player board, plus twenty-one to fill every depletion grid in play,
  so a table trading five finite commodities gets through a hundred in a game. They are not
  recycled and there is no reserve: running out of pips is running out of seam, which is
  exactly the right thing for it to mean.
- **Route tokens** — road and rail, laid on the map hex by hex, and the piece that puts a
  player's name on what they paid for. A bar the length of the line between two hex
  centres: at the default four-sheet Korvane Reach board that is **15 × 3.7 mm** for road
  and **15 × 5.3 mm** for rail, and `tools/build-map.mjs` prints the figures for every
  print preset, because the size is the map's business and not the token's. Ownership is
  peg holes down the centre line — one for the first player, two for the second — as well
  as colour, so the toll (see [economy](02-economy.md)) is never an argument.
- **Round marker**, turn order markers, first player token

## Mini-maps

One A4 landscape sheet per terrain: a hexagon of 61 hexagonal cells — the inside of a
single campaign-map cell — with an encounter panel and a holdings panel in the space a
hexagon on a rectangle leaves over.

**A mini-map is a colour, a pattern and a grid.** No render and no plate. The field is the
colour that terrain already prints in (`palette.json terrain[].wash`), patterned with that
terrain's own **map mark** — a grass tuft, a conifer, two hummocks, a peak, reeds, a frost
star, a dune, a river between its banks — with the grid ruled on top, and that is the whole
sheet. It still needs no artist and no place in the mint queue.

The sheet was a flat colour and nothing else, on the argument that drawn ground competes
with the pieces standing on it, and every one of these sheets is a sheet somebody is
standing pieces on. Half right: a *drawn* sheet competes. A mark at a third of a cell, on
the ink plate at a third of full strength — quieter than the grid ruled over it — does not,
and it does what the colour could not do alone: say which ground this is from across the
table, and say it in the black-and-white edition too. The marks are the world map's own,
carried as data on the terrain (`terrain.json terrains[].mark`) exactly as an element
carries its mark, and drawn per `components.json marks.terrain`.

**A cell is a world hex — exactly.** 16.7 mm at the default four-sheet print preset, read
off the map rather than chosen, so a figure based for the campaign board stands in a
mini-map cell without being re-based, a route token cut for one fits the other, and one
ruler measures both. Print the map bigger and the sheets follow. The scale is a fiction —
the ground inside one hex is not sixty-one hexes of ground — and it is the right fiction,
because everything physical about the two boards agrees.

`tools/build-minimaps.mjs` generates them into [`docs/minimaps/sheets/`](../minimaps/index.html);
the spec is [`docs/minimaps/README.md`](../minimaps/README.md) and `data/minimap.json`. The
thirty-two hand-drawn sheets that preceded this — Holdings, Grounds and Places, part of the
shelved tile board ([#18](https://github.com/cdomotor-g/game1/issues/18)) — stay committed
at [`docs/minimaps/img/`](../minimaps/img/) and are not touched.

## Print-and-play

Everything above is derivable from `data/*.json`, so a generator that renders card
fronts from the data is an obvious next tool — and it would keep the printed components
honest against the rules automatically, in the same way the web explorer already is.
Several slices of it exist. `tools/build-cards.mjs` renders the adventure decks —
characters, vehicles, monsters and talismans — into [`docs/cards/`](../cards/index.html)
from the data and the accepted plates in `docs/art/renders/`, one card per accepted
render, print page included. `tools/build-board.mjs` renders the player board into
[`docs/boards/`](../boards/index.html), `tools/build-market.mjs` the market board **and the
depletion sheet** into [`docs/markets/`](../markets/index.html), `tools/build-ledger.mjs`
**the price ledger** into [`docs/ledger/`](../ledger/index.html), and
`tools/build-minimaps.mjs` the mini-map sheets into
[`docs/minimaps/`](../minimaps/index.html) — every one at true A4, and the ledger the only
one of them in portrait.

None of them holds a coordinate it did not work out, and the useful test of that is what
each one does when something gets bigger.

The player board's track columns are whatever width is left once the card recesses and the
wear ladders have had theirs — so **a bigger pip makes wider wear ladders and narrower
columns**, in that order, and the build fails before a column gets too narrow for a bar. It
is the same arithmetic that made a sixth track narrow the columns and retiring two of them
hand the width back.

The ledger is the sharpest of them, because its ceiling is not a piece, it is the length of
the game. A row is the working height over `rules.json victory.gameLengthRounds`, so **a
longer game makes shorter rows, and shorter rows make smaller digits**: 24 rounds gives an
8.4 mm digit, 25 gives exactly 8.0 and is the last one that passes, and 26 gives 7.63 and
**fails the build, naming the game length as the cause**. Below 8 mm a segment's colourable
core is thinner than a pencil stroke and the sheet stops being fillable, which is not a
tolerance worth arguing with. A longer game wants a second sheet, not smaller figures.

Both of those replaced an older trade of exactly the same shape, and it is worth recording
because it was the one this document used to point at. A market line was tally, memory and
price ladder side by side, each strip cell a bar token wide, so widening the memory track
narrowed the price ladder — and widening it far enough failed the build rather than printing
a band cell a hexagon overhung. There is no memory strip and no ladder now, so that
particular squeeze does not exist any more. What squeezes instead is the pip against the
player board's columns and the game length against the ledger's digits — and, on the
depletion sheet, the pip a second time: a cell is the pip plus its clearance and a grid is
`per` cells across by `top` + 1 rows down, so a bigger pip makes bigger cells, wider grids
and fewer of them per sheet, in that order. A mini-map cell is a world hex, so the field
sizes itself and a field that would not fit the paper fails the build.

The [two-plate system](../art/01-two-plate-system.md) is what makes that generator
tractable: one layout pass emits a `#wash` group and an `#ink` group, the colour edition
is both, and the print-and-play edition is the ink group alone. Not two design efforts —
one layout and two print runs. The files in [../art/examples/](../art/examples/) are built
to that contract and are the generator's target output.
