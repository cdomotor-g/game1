# 00 — Art direction

## The one-line version

**A working settlement's trade almanac, printed on a tired press, carried in a pocket for
a season, and handed to you with the dirt still on it.**

Everything in the game is a page out of that book.

## Why that conceit and not "fantasy game art"

The game is about hours, ledgers, chains and wear. It is a game where you count things
and where your axe breaks. An art direction of heroic vistas and glowing hero portraits
would be lying about what the player actually does.

So the world is not depicted — it is **documented**. Every commodity chit is a catalogue
entry. Every building card is a plate from a builder's manual. Every event card is a
notice nailed to a post. The player is not looking at the world; they are looking at the
paperwork the world generates, which is exactly what they are doing mechanically.

This buys us four things at once:

1. **It is print-native.** A book printed badly is the aesthetic, so press artefacts,
   limited inks and cheap paper are features rather than compromises.
2. **It scales to an enormous parts list.** 63 commodities, 83 recipes, 51 buildings and
   58 event cards is a lot of art. A catalogue has a house style; a gallery does not.
3. **It makes black-and-white the honest mode**, not the degraded one. See
   [01-two-plate-system.md](01-two-plate-system.md).
4. **It is ours.** Nobody owns "printed almanac". Plenty of people own their fantasy
   worlds. See [08-influences-and-distance.md](08-influences-and-distance.md).

## The three words

The brief was *dirty, rustic, magical*. They are not equal partners and they do not blend
— each one owns a different layer of the image.

### Dirty — owns the surface

Dirt is on the **paper and the press**, not on the subject. The plate was inked
unevenly. The corner over-inked and filled in. Somebody's thumb was wet. There is a ring
where a cup sat. The fold has worn through.

This is the crucial distinction. If the dirt is painted onto the objects — mud smeared on
a barrel — it reads as a texture-brush and it makes every asset noisier than the last. If
the dirt lives on the surface of the print, it is consistent across every component for
free, and it never competes with the linework.

> **Rule:** grime is a layer over the whole page, at one strength, applied last. It is
> never part of an object's own drawing.

### Rustic — owns the construction

Everything in this world is **made by hand out of a small number of materials**: sawn
timber, hammered iron, fired clay, rough cloth, rope. Fixings are visible — rivets,
straps, pegs, stitching, lashings. Nothing is moulded, extruded, seamless or injection
made. If you cannot see how a thing was assembled, redraw it.

The same rule governs the graphic furniture. Card borders are plank-and-strap, not
filigree. Dividers are rope or chain. Corners are iron banding with square rivets. The
frame is built from the same materials as the world inside it.

### Magical — owns the exception

Magic is not a style applied to magical things. It is **a rule the printing process
cannot obey**.

There are two mana veins on the entire board. Mana is the scarcest thing in the game, and
the art must make that visible before a player has read a single number. So the arcane
gets exactly one visual privilege, and nothing else does:

> **The plate slips.** On arcane subjects — and only arcane subjects — the colour is
> printed fractionally out of register, so it escapes past the black line and ghosts
> beyond the edge of the thing it belongs to.

Diegetically, the almanac's printer could never get magic to sit still on the page.
Mechanically, it costs nothing, it is unmistakable at a glance across a table, and it
degrades perfectly: in black-and-white mode there is no slip, because there is no colour
to slip, and no information is lost.

No glows. No sparkles. No particle effects. No radial light. Magic is a misprint.

## Tone

The settlement is **working, not doomed**. This is the most common way to get the mood
wrong.

| Yes | No |
|---|---|
| Worn because it is used daily | Ruined, abandoned, post-apocalyptic |
| Patched and still in service | Broken and left where it fell |
| Practical, cheap, effective | Grimdark, ashen, hopeless |
| Dry humour in the margins | Jokes in the artwork itself |
| Somebody's livelihood | Somebody's tragedy |

There is misery available — the event deck contains plague, raiders and famine — but the
default state of the world is a place where people got up and went to work. A tool in the
art should look like it was sharpened last week, not like it was dug out of a grave.

Humour belongs in **the writing in the margins**, not in the drawing: a printer's
annotation, a crossed-out price, a note that reads *"third pressing — the first two were
eaten."* The pictures play it straight. This keeps the game funny without making any
single asset a joke that stops being funny the fortieth time it comes up.

## Motifs

Six recurring devices. Every asset should contain at least one; most contain two. They
are what makes 300 separate pieces of art look like one game.

**1. The tally.** Effort is hours, and hours are counted in scratched five-bar gates.
Any quantity that is spent rather than owned is drawn as tally marks — effort costs,
round tracks, wear. It ties the single most important mechanic to a mark a player can
recognise from across the room.

**2. The maker's mark.** Every building, tool and manufactured good carries a small
stamped touchmark, the way a smith stamps their work. It gives 150 assets a shared piece
of graphic vocabulary and a natural place to put an identifying glyph.

**3. The chain.** Production chains are the spine of the game. Anything showing a
sequence is joined with actual links, rope or lashing — never a plain arrow.

**4. Timber and iron banding.** The frame device. Rough sawn plank with hammered iron
straps and square rivets at the corners. Used on card borders, panel edges and the
player board.

**5. Wear.** Nothing is new. Tools have notched durability, edges are chipped, rope is
frayed, cloth is patched. Wear is a mechanic — tools break — so it should be legible in
the art, not decorative.

**6. The annotation.** A previous owner has been in this book. Prices crossed out and
rewritten, a margin note, a corner folded down, an ink smudge where they wrote too soon.
This is where the voice of the game lives.

## What this is not

Non-negotiable exclusions, because each of these is a well-worn road to somewhere we do
not want to be:

- **Not neon.** No colour that only exists on a screen. Every ink must be mixable and
  printable. If it cannot be a real pigment, it is not in the game.
- **Not gradients.** Volume comes from hatching. No airbrush, no glow, no drop shadow.
- **Not photorealistic, and not painterly.** It is printed line art. If it looks like an
  oil painting or a render, it has left the conceit.
- **Not cartoon-cute.** Chunky and exaggerated, yes. Rounded, big-eyed and friendly, no.
- **Not grimdark.** See tone, above.
- **Not busy.** A commodity chit is 18mm across. Silhouette first, detail second, and
  detail only if it survives at 18mm.
